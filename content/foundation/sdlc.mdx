## What this lesson covers

The **software development life cycle** is how an idea becomes running software — and how it stays running. Testers who only appear at the end inherit everyone else's ambiguity.

- Common SDLC models and what they optimize
- Where testing belongs in each phase
- Shift-left habits that still respect delivery dates

## Outcome

After this lesson you will be able to point to a story, a design, or a release plan and say what QA should do **now**, not just after code-complete.

## Models you will actually meet

| Model | Cadence | QA implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Waterfall / V-model | Long phases, late integration | Formal entry/exit; test basis locked early |
| Iterative / incremental | Slices of value | Regression grows every slice |
| Scrum / Kanban | Continuous flow | Testers in the team; definition of done includes test evidence |
| Continuous delivery | Many small releases | Automation carries the boring path; humans target new risk |

<Info title="The V-model in one sentence">
Each development activity has a matching test activity: requirements map to acceptance, architecture to system tests, detailed design to integration, code to component tests.
</Info>

You will rarely run a textbook V-model. You will still use the mapping: if nobody wrote acceptance examples, you do not have a test basis for "done."

## Where QA adds leverage

```text
Idea → story → design → code → build → release → operate
         ^        ^               ^        ^         ^
     ambiguity  testability   fast fail  evidence  production signals
```

- **Stories:** challenge missing actors, error paths, and data rules.
- **Design:** ask which states, roles, and integrations are in scope.
- **Code/build:** pair on testability (ids, logs, feature flags).
- **Release:** confirm the slice of risk you claimed to cover.
- **Operate:** feed incidents back into the next test charter.

<Warning title="Hardening weeks are a smell">
A calendar block named "QA week" usually means testing was treated as a phase, not a skill. Use it as a signal to pull test design earlier next sprint — do not make it your identity.
</Warning>

## Practical example: a payment story

Story: "As a shopper I can pay with a saved card so checkout is faster."

Shift-left questions a tester asks **before** code:

- What happens when the saved card is expired?
- Can a shopper have zero cards, one card, or many?
- Is the card tokenized, or is PAN in our database?
- Which environments have a real payment sandbox?

Those questions are cheaper than finding a PCI surprise in staging.
